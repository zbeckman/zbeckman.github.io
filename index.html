Makerspace Access Control

An ID-swipe system that locks each shop tool to the students trained on it, in a makerspace serving more than 2,000 students.

Role
Originated, designed and built; leading rollout
Dates
May 2026 – present
Tools
ESP32-S3, RFID, Raspberry Pi, current sensing, Onshape, C/C++
Status
In progress

Problem

Tool access wasn't restricted to trained users. That was a safety exposure on the higher-risk machines and a throughput problem, since staff had to gatekeep tools by hand. I identified the gap and proposed the system.

What I did

  • Prototyped the sign-in on an Arduino, then moved to an ESP32 with an RFID reader. The first architecture relied on a USB Host FeatherWing (MAX3421E) that proved incompatible with the ESP32-WROOM-32D. I ruled out wiring and firmware on the bench before finding the cause.
  • Re-architected around an ESP32-S3 with native USB OTG using TinyUSB Host, which removed the external host controller entirely.
  • Added current sensing at each controlled outlet, so the system records actual tool run time as well as badge-ins.
  • Designed a Raspberry Pi hub that syncs the authorized-user list from Smartsheet and caches it at each tool, so tools keep working through a network outage.
  • Built the front-desk kiosk: students tap their BuckID to sign in, staff grant tool certifications in two taps, and a dashboard tracks visits, busy hours and tool time.
  • Led the bill of materials and component sourcing for the rollout.
Result

The sign-in kiosk is live at the front desk with a control panel for 10 tools, and tool reader rollout is underway. The usage data it collects now supports the department's funding requests, which was never part of the original scope.

Omnidirectional Garden Robot

A kiwi-drive competition robot that placed 3rd of more than 60 teams with a perfect design score.

Role
Design team member
Dates
Feb – May 2025
Tools
Proteus controller, PID control, CAD
Status
Complete

Problem

The course demanded precise maneuvering in a confined plot, where a two-wheel drive would spend much of its time repositioning.

What I did

  • Generated three chassis concepts before committing: two wheels with skids and a mechanical arm, a three-wheel omnidirectional base, and a belt-driven lift. Each sketch calls out drive, sensing and actuation so the team could compare them on the same terms.
  • Converged on a kiwi drive: three omniwheels at 120°, which translates in any direction and rotates independently of heading.
  • Modeled the chassis, drivetrain and lift arm in CAD, then built the platform on a recycled plastic base with a wooden arm mechanism.
  • Ran PID control on the Proteus controller and tuned the loop against the robot's real mechanical response.
Result

3rd place out of more than 60 teams, with a 100/100 design score.

Aimable Ballista

A joystick-aimed, Arduino-controlled launcher built from printed gears and laser-cut panels.

Role
Sole designer and builder
Dates
Aug – Dec 2025
Tools
Onshape, 3D printing, laser cutting, Arduino
Status
Complete

Problem

Carry real spring load through printed parts while keeping the aiming axes smooth and repeatable under motor control.

What I did

  • Modeled the full assembly in CAD, then ran the loop: print or cut a part, test the fit and load, find what the model got wrong, revise. That cycle ran for most of the semester.
  • Designed a printed gear train for the pan axis, a large ring gear driven by a pinion, so the turret aims under motor control. Getting printed teeth to mesh cleanly took several revisions.
  • Built the structure from PLA parts and laser-cut panels, revising geometry where parts deflected or tolerances stacked across the aiming joints.
  • Wired and programmed the controls myself: stepper and servo motors, joystick input, an LCD and LED indicators on an Arduino.
Result

A fully aimable, working launcher. It's the project that taught me a design is finished when the part can be made and still fits, which is later than when the model looks right.

Makerspace Partition

A 50-foot mobile partition separating the power-tool zone from the rest of the makerspace, built for a fifth of the commercial quote.

Role
Sole designer and builder
Dates
Jun – Aug 2026
Tools
Onshape, 80/20 T-slot aluminum, 3D printing
Status
Complete

Problem

The space needed a split between the dusty power tools, where safety glasses are required, and the clear table area, across 30 ft and 20 ft openings. A commercial mobile partition was quoted at about $1,500.

What I did

  • Designed the system in CAD around 80/20 T-slot extrusion, with A-frame legs and casters for stability across the span.
  • Designed and 3D printed custom gussets for every frame joint.
  • Built the bill of materials around existing shop inventory and stock lengths, and reworked the design until it could be built mostly from material on hand.
Result

About $300 in materials against the $1,500 quote. Built and in use.

Solar Irrigation System

Solar-powered drip irrigation for the 24 container gardens at Ohio State's North Campus Community Garden.

Role
Project lead, team of 10–15
Dates
Sept 2025 – present
Tools
Electrical design, ESP controller, site layout, CAD
Status
In progress

Problem

Campus sustainability staff asked Engineers for a Sustainable World to design and build irrigation for the garden's 24 container beds, which were watered by hand.

What I did

  • Lead a team of 10 to 15 students meeting weekly, breaking the system into pieces people can own so they keep coming back.
  • Completed the electrical design: solar panel, charge controller, battery, ESP controller, relay and valve, with soil moisture sensing so zones open on measured conditions instead of a timer.
  • Produced the site layout for all 24 beds, routing tubing, splitters and emitters from a single spigot.
  • Sketched control-box concepts against a short list of goals: cheap, simple to build, weatherproof, and able to survive public handling.
  • Coordinated with OSU Facilities and am leading a grant application for funding.
Status

2025–26 was research and system selection. 2026–27 is design and build in the garden, with the grant application underway.

Rainwater Harvesting, Guatemala

A 15,000-liter rainwater system for a 240-member agricultural cooperative near Lake Atitlán.

Role
Senior-capstone team member
Dates
Jan – Apr 2026
Location
Sololá, Guatemala
Status
Complete

Problem

The cooperative needed a reliable water supply for its fertilizer production. Our team designed the system in Columbus, then traveled to install it.

What I did

  • Contributed to the system design and bill of materials, then worked the in-country build and installation.
  • The design called for 30° elbows at the tank connections. Only 45° elbows were available locally, so we switched to the alternate layout we had prepared and adjusted pipe runs on site.
  • The lesson I took: what you can actually buy where you're building is a design input.
Result

Supplies the cooperative's fertilizer production, with an estimated $6,400 to $12,800 in annual economic impact. It has collected water since installation.
